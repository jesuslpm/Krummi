Query profile sampling

How the query profile keeps every execution in exact aggregates and a sample of executions as individual rows, and why the sample's probability has the shape it has. It accompanies 15-query-profile-and-tuning.md, sections Aggregates and Sampled executions. The curves are expected values computed from the design's formulas, not a random simulation. Where lines coincide they are drawn a few pixels apart, so that none hides another; hover a chart to read the exact values, and each chart has a data table below it.

Two records of every execution

When a query ends, its thread writes one small record into a ring of its own and goes on. One consumer thread drains every ring and keeps the two records below, so the query's thread shares nothing it writes. The query's thread only draws whether the execution is also kept as a row, with a probability the consumer publishes, and adds the parameter values when it is.

The aggregates give exact totals: how many executions, how much time and how many pages, which is what the ranking of the costliest queries and the validation after a change need. The sampled rows give detail that totals hide: which parameter values are slow, what happened at 10:42, how one plan behaves against another. The rest of this page is about the sampler: how often it keeps a row, and why.

How large a sample needs to be

A sample's precision depends on its size, not on the size of the population it comes from. To estimate a proportion within ±2 % at 95 % confidence, about 2,400 rows are enough whether the shape ran ten thousand times or a billion. Below a few thousand executions the sample is the whole population: everything is kept.

So keeping a fixed fraction of executions is wasteful: a shape that runs a billion times a day would keep millions of rows and gain no precision. The probability of keeping a row must fall as a shape runs more often. That is the job of T: roughly how many rows a shape keeps per day.

A new entry: its probability over time

An entry is a (shape, plan) pair; a new plan of an old shape is a new entry, so a plan regression gets detail at once. The probability of keeping an execution of an entry is

p = g · min( R / c, min( p_max, max( p_min, T / n ) ) )
  • n is the entry's count of recent executions. It decays: at each flush it is multiplied by e^(−Δt/τ), so it approximates the executions of the last τ (a day) and never restarts.
  • T / n keeps the first T executions of a new entry (with p_max = 1) and then falls as the entry runs more often; p_min is its floor.
  • R / c aims the entry at R kept rows per second, where c is its executions per second. It is a target of the expected rate, not a hard quota, and it brakes only an entry that runs more than R times per second: within such an entry's first second c is still small, so it keeps more than R rows that second. An entry that runs R times per second or fewer has R / c ≥ 1 and is never braked by it.
  • g ≤ 1 is a global factor that aims the whole store at a budget of B rows per second, also a target of the expected rate: it follows the demand one second late (last chart).

The chart compares three ways of counting for one new entry that runs at a constant rate.

  • Counter since the first execution (blue): keeps everything, then falls as T/n forever, down to p_min. Restarted every day for every shape, it is the scheme that floods the profile at midnight (last chart).
  • Decayed count (orange): identical at the start, then flattens at T/(rate·τ) once the count reaches a day's worth of executions. A steady entry keeps about T rows a day, spread evenly through the day.
  • Decayed count with the cap R (green): an entry that runs more than R times per second keeps, after its first second, min(R, rate·p) rows per second, where p is the orange curve's probability: about R while its first T rows last, so they spread over minutes instead of a fraction of a second, and fewer later. Within the first second its expected rate starts at the entry's full rate and falls as R/c, keeping at most about R(1 + ln(rate/R)) rows that second. A slower entry follows the orange curve: the cap does not act on it.

What that means in rows

Rows kept per second are the entry's rate times p. Without the cap, a new entry keeps everything at its full rate until it reaches T; with it, the start of an entry that runs more than R times per second is flattened after its first second to about R rows per second (above R within that first second, as above), and a slower entry keeps the same rows as without the cap. In the engine the consumer also publishes the probability once per tick, so a new entry keeps up to one tick of rows with p = 1 before the cap acts; the charts leave that delay out. On the right, the rows kept since the start: a new entry keeps more in its first τ, then about T per τ.

The whole store: restarts, spikes and the budget

Now 500 shapes with rates that follow a power law (a few very frequent, many rare), over three days. The process starts at hour 0. The chart shows the rows the profile receives per second, in five-minute bins.

  • Counter restarted every day (blue): every shape is back at p = 1 at the same moment, so each midnight brings a burst orders of magnitude above the steady level, with the buffers filling exactly then.
  • Decayed count (orange): no daily burst; only the start of the process, when every count is zero, still spikes.
  • With the cap R (green): the start spike is lower, since after its first second no entry keeps more than R expected rows per second.
  • With the budget B (yellow): the factor g lowers every probability before the draw whenever the expected demand passes B, so after its first second the start of the process stays near the budget. This curve is an idealization: it clips the demand at B, while g, computed from the previous second and smoothed, lets the first second of a spike pass B.

The rule behind the last two: brake before the draw, never after. A limiter that throws away rows already drawn depends on the order of arrival, so the real probability of a kept row becomes unknown. A factor applied before the draw is known and stored with the row. Only the memory bound of the buffers may still drop rows; if it does, the interval is marked degraded.

Why every row carries its probability

A row kept with probability p stands for 1/p executions: kept with p = 1 it stands for itself, with p = 0.001 for a thousand. Since the probability varies (a new entry starts high, a restart starts everything again, g brakes a spike), an unweighted sample over-represents the moments when it was high.

Example from the design: a new shape whose first 1,000 executions take 50 ms (a cold cache) and whose next 999,000 take 1 ms, sampled with T = 1,000. The sample holds the 1,000 slow executions and about 6,900 fast ones.

Weighted by 1/p, the sample gives back the true mean and p95. The weights are then scaled so that they add up to the exact execution count from the aggregates, which lowers the variance at no cost.

Where the duration histogram comes from

The aggregates cannot store averages or percentiles, because those do not merge across threads, intervals and days. They store a histogram instead, built from the executions themselves: each execution adds 1 to the bucket its duration (or page count) falls in. The bucket limits are fixed in advance and identical everywhere, so two histograms merge by adding their counters.

octave = 63 - leading_zeros(x)            // the power of two that holds x
sub    = (x >> (octave - k)) & (2^k - 1)  // the k bits after the highest one
bucket = octave * 2^k + sub

Each power of two is cut into 2^k equal buckets, with bit arithmetic only. A percentile is read at its bucket's midpoint, so its error is at most half a bucket. The chart shows that worst error for every value: a sawtooth that restarts at each power of two.

The design picks k = 4: at most 3.1 %, and about 560 possible buckets for durations between 100 ns and an hour, of which only the non-empty ones are stored.
